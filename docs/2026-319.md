## 13:38 MST

### A stable shape

The interface finally has a shape I like, and it doesn't change between pages. A
panel on the left to navigate, one on the right for whatever is selected, and
one across the bottom for data over time. The rest of the screen is the model.
Pages just fill the panels.

### The model is the navigator

This is the part I'm most happy about. The model is always there and never swaps
out, selecting something just moves the camera. When you open Nanta you see
space as it is right now: the Sun in the middle, and Earth, the Moon, and Mars
where they actually are. It's one Horizons call per body, and then the clock
moves them.

Pick a body and the camera flies to it. Pick a site and it lands on the skyline,
with the site's up as up. Before that, the south pole looked upside down, which
made no sense if you're the one standing there.

The directions are real. They come from Horizons and each body's rotation, so
the Sun and the Earth are where you'd actually see them from the site, and the
Sun lights everything from the right side. Sizes and distances aren't to scale,
I tried true scale and everything was too small to see. I wrote down which parts
are real and which are just drawn to fit.

I also added the brightest stars, and the four on the ecliptic that mark where
the planets move, as a reference. And a UTC clock in the header, because time
matters a lot here, plus a small readout of the camera angle so I know what I'm
looking at.

### Conditions drive the data

Each site now says what it's judged on: sunlight, which is power, and Earth
visibility, which is a direct link home. That decides what we ask Horizons for
and what gets drawn. Moon sites have both, Earth and Mars only sunlight for now.
The right panel shows each condition at the selected time, and the bottom shows
a curve per condition, kind of like a weather forecast.

### Colors from the Andes

The accent is now cochineal, the dye behind the reds and magentas in Paracas and
Inca textiles, and in quipu cords. It fits the name and the woven path of the N
in the logo. Blue now only means the Earth.

## 14:44 MST

### Understanding the horizon

I stopped for a bit to really understand what "relative to the horizon" means.
It's simple once it clicks: the horizon is where the ground meets the sky from
where you stand. If the Sun is above it, there's power. If the Earth is above
it, you can talk home. Below it, the ground is in the way.

The tricky part is that there are two horizons. The ideal one is flat, like the
Moon was a perfect ball, and that's what Horizons gives me. The real one is the
skyline, crater rims and ridges. At the south pole the Sun barely gets above a
degree, so a small ridge can mean days without light. That's the whole reason
small changes in place or date matter so much. For now Nanta uses the ideal
horizon, and I wrote down that terrain is the next big step.

### The horizon chart

The 3D view was not good for this. A Sun at -0.1° and one at +0.1° look the
same, and that's the difference between no power and some. So I added a horizon
chart on the right: compass direction across, elevation up, zoomed so one degree
is easy to see. The ground below the line is shaded, the Sun is drawn at its real
size, so you can actually see it half cut by the horizon. You can point at the
path to pick a time and everything follows. I think this is the most useful
piece so far.

### Sites you can add

Sites now have short code names, APL11, CY3, SCR, which fits better with
comparing. I also checked every predefined site against public sources and left
a note on where each one comes from. The Blue Moon MK1 site is a ridge near
Shackleton, but there's no exact point published yet, so I marked it as a
placeholder.

The bigger change is that you can add your own site now. There's a + next to the
body, a small form with a code name, latitude, longitude, radius, and what to
look for, sunlight or Earth visibility. As you type it shows on the model, and
you can also just click the body to pick the point. They're saved in the
browser for now, Workers can't write files and I don't want a database yet.

### A couple of rules

The accent is only ever a line, never a solid fill. Focus outlines are drawn
inside the element so a scroll area never cuts them, and since nothing is filled
with the accent, they always show. Simple rule, fewer problems.
