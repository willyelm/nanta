## 13:38 MST

### A stable shape

The interface finally has a shape I like, and it doesn't change between pages. A
panel on the left to navigate, one on the right for whatever is selected, and
one across the bottom for data over time. The rest of the screen is the model.
Pages just fill the panels.

### The model is the navigator

This is the part I'm most happy about. The model is always there and never swaps
out, selecting something just moves the camera. When you open Nanta you see
space as it is right now: the Sun in the middle, and Earth, the Moon, and Mars
where they actually are. It's one Horizons call per body, and then the clock
moves them.

Pick a body and the camera flies to it. Pick a site and it lands on the skyline,
with the site's up as up. Before that, the south pole looked upside down, which
made no sense if you're the one standing there.

The directions are real. They come from Horizons and each body's rotation, so
the Sun and the Earth are where you'd actually see them from the site, and the
Sun lights everything from the right side. Sizes and distances aren't to scale,
I tried true scale and everything was too small to see. I wrote down which parts
are real and which are just drawn to fit.

I also added the brightest stars, and the four on the ecliptic that mark where
the planets move, as a reference. And a UTC clock in the header, because time
matters a lot here, plus a small readout of the camera angle so I know what I'm
looking at.

### Conditions drive the data

Each site now says what it's judged on: sunlight, which is power, and Earth
visibility, which is a direct link home. That decides what we ask Horizons for
and what gets drawn. Moon sites have both, Earth and Mars only sunlight for now.
The right panel shows each condition at the selected time, and the bottom shows
a curve per condition, kind of like a weather forecast.

### Colors from the Andes

The accent is now cochineal, the dye behind the reds and magentas in Paracas and
Inca textiles, and in quipu cords. It fits the name and the woven path of the N
in the logo. Blue now only means the Earth.

## 14:44 MST

### Understanding the horizon

I stopped for a bit to really understand what "relative to the horizon" means.
It's simple once it clicks: the horizon is where the ground meets the sky from
where you stand. If the Sun is above it, there's power. If the Earth is above
it, you can talk home. Below it, the ground is in the way.

The tricky part is that there are two horizons. The ideal one is flat, like the
Moon was a perfect ball, and that's what Horizons gives me. The real one is the
skyline, crater rims and ridges. At the south pole the Sun barely gets above a
degree, so a small ridge can mean days without light. That's the whole reason
small changes in place or date matter so much. For now Nanta uses the ideal
horizon, and I wrote down that terrain is the next big step.

### The horizon chart

The 3D view was not good for this. A Sun at -0.1° and one at +0.1° look the
same, and that's the difference between no power and some. So I added a horizon
chart on the right: compass direction across, elevation up, zoomed so one degree
is easy to see. The ground below the line is shaded, the Sun is drawn at its real
size, so you can actually see it half cut by the horizon. You can point at the
path to pick a time and everything follows. I think this is the most useful
piece so far.

### Sites you can add

Sites now have short code names, APL11, CY3, SCR, which fits better with
comparing. I also checked every predefined site against public sources and left
a note on where each one comes from. The Blue Moon MK1 site is a ridge near
Shackleton, but there's no exact point published yet, so I marked it as a
placeholder.

The bigger change is that you can add your own site now. There's a + next to the
body, a small form with a code name, latitude, longitude, radius, and what to
look for, sunlight or Earth visibility. As you type it shows on the model, and
you can also just click the body to pick the point. They're saved in the
browser for now, Workers can't write files and I don't want a database yet.

### A couple of rules

The accent is only ever a line, never a solid fill. Focus outlines are drawn
inside the element so a scroll area never cuts them, and since nothing is filled
with the accent, they always show. Simple rule, fewer problems.

## 16:15 MST

### A few hours of design

The last few hours were mostly design, and honestly I enjoyed them more than
anything else so far. Earlier today I really thought I would not finish. There
were too many pieces and none of them fit together yet. Now I'm a lot more
confident I can deliver something solid.

It started with the horizon. I had to stop and actually understand what
"relative to the horizon" means, and once it clicked, the 3D view was not
enough anymore. A Sun at -0.1° and one at +0.1° look exactly the same in 3D,
and that's the difference between no power and some. So I drew it flat, compass
across and elevation up, with the Sun at its real size. Seeing half the Sun cut
by the horizon line at the south pole was the moment it felt right.

Then the sites. I gave them short code names, checked every one against a
public source, and made it possible to add my own, with a form or just by
clicking on the body. I tried giving sites a radius, then realized Horizons
answers for a point, not an area, so I dropped it. A lot of the afternoon was
like that, adding something, looking at it, and taking it back out. The UI got
simpler every time.

The layout went through a few rounds too. Grid, then flex, then panels that
size to their content. Back buttons that go up one level, not just home. Small
things, like the accent only ever being a ring and never a fill, so focus is
always visible. Each rule removed a problem instead of adding one.

### The model that settled it

And then the last piece fell into place. Sites and windows are two separate
things:

- A site is a place: a body, a point on it, and what it's judged on.
- A window is a time: a range of dates with its own code, not tied to a site.
- A candidate is a plan: one site and one window together.

The left side is where: bodies, sites, a site. The right side is when and
what: windows, then sunlight and Earth visibility for that time. Keeping them
apart means I can mix and match. Same site in another window is a launch slip.
Same window at another site is a moved landing. When a pair looks good, I save
it as a candidate, and then I compare candidates.

That's exactly what the challenge asks for, and it fits the screen I already
have. It feels like the design finally stopped moving.

### What's left

- Save a site and a window as a candidate, and list them.
- Compare up to three candidates on the same horizon chart and timeline.

It's a clear finish line, and for the first time today I think I'll get there.

## 22:14 MST

### Cleanup, and then more than cleanup

I meant to only clean up. I removed about a hundred icons I never used, and a
few components. Then I checked whether the model was actually right, and it
wasn't, not everywhere. Mars sites had never loaded: Horizons wants Mars
longitudes as negative numbers, and every request was being refused. The Moon
was tilted wrong too. I had left out its wobble, and at the south pole that's
the whole Sun: Horizons said 1.3° up, the model said below the horizon. Now the
scene matches Horizons within a few tenths of a degree, and every body turns
the way it really does.

### Pictures of the place

The reading has a third column now: pictures of the site, with a ring on the
exact point. Moon Trek for the Moon, Mars Trek for Mars, and for Earth a Blue
Marble map and the day's satellite picture. The compass moved on top of the raw
numbers, with a line saying when Horizons was asked.

I also added Mercury, which was easier than I expected, and one more place on
Earth: Inti Punku, the Sun Gate at Machu Picchu. That one is personal. Growing
up in Peru, I wondered how people studied the sky before telescopes, and the
Inca built gates and roads around the Sun. That wonder is what first made me
think about space, and it's why the project has a Quechua name.

### Compare like a game

Comparing finally feels right. Each candidate in the bottom bar has a chevron,
and hovering it shows a diff against what I'm looking at, like comparing armor
in a game: minus is the candidate, plus is the current one, green for more of a
condition and red for less. It never says better or worse, only more or less.
It's plain text, so it can be pasted anywhere. Link and unlink became pin and
unpin, which is what it really is.

### Wrapping up

That's it for features. I'm happy with where it landed. Now the slides,
screenshots and short recordings for the spec, and the submission before
midnight.
